The Andersen No-Sway System

How Andersen's revolutionary chain-and-friction-cone design replaces traditional spring bars with a quieter, lighter, and more effective solution.

🔩

Friction Cone Anti-Sway

A tapered ball and brake-pad-material friction housing create self-adjusting sway resistance. The heavier the tongue load, the more friction is applied — automatically. No separate sway bars or friction controllers to adjust.

⛓️

Motion-Dampening Chains

Instead of spring bars that act like diving boards (storing and releasing kinetic energy as bounce), Andersen's chains pull at a right angle to vertical movement, with urethane springs that absorb road shock and eliminate porpoising.

🪶

Under 60 lbs Total Weight

The entire system — shank, ball, chains, and frame brackets — weighs less than 60 lbs. Traditional spring-bar WDH systems can weigh 80–100+ lbs. Lighter weight means easier hookup, less strain, and more payload capacity for your gear.

🔄

Backup-Friendly

Unlike many traditional WDH systems with add-on friction sway bars, the Andersen system allows you to back up without disconnecting anything. This is invaluable when maneuvering in tight mountain campgrounds and trailheads.

🧼

Grease-Free Design

The ball and coupler move as a single unit inside the friction housing — no grease required. Clean hands, clean clothes, no mess at the campsite. The ball rotates with the coupler rather than against it.

⚡

Quick 4-Step Hookup

Lock coupler → raise tongue jack → connect D-ring shackles to chain plate → lower jack. That's it. Same tension every time once dialed in. No pry bars, no remembering chain links, no heavy lifting.

Rocky Mountain Towing Challenges

The Rockies present the most demanding towing environment in the lower 48. Here's why a quality WDH system is not optional — it's essential.

🏔️ High Elevation Power Loss

The Ford 7.3L gas V8 loses approximately 3–4% of its power for every 1,000 feet above sea level. At Colorado's high passes (10,000–11,500 ft), that translates to a 30–46% power reduction. Less power means slower climbs, more transmission shifting, and increased heat — all while managing a 5,000–6,000 lb trailer. Every pound of unnecessary trailer sway compounds the engine's workload.

💨 Valley Crosswinds & Gusts

Rocky Mountain valleys and high plains (Wyoming's I-80, Colorado's South Park, Montana's front range) are notorious for sustained 30–50 mph crosswinds. An enclosed trailer acts as a giant sail — the high, flat sides catch wind and create lateral force on the tow vehicle. Without sway resistance, gusts can push the trailer (and motorhome) half a lane or more.

📐 Steep Sustained Grades

I-70 through Colorado features 6–8% grades lasting 5–10 miles. The descent from the Eisenhower Tunnel (11,158 ft) to Silverthorne drops over 2,000 feet in just a few miles. Steep descents with a heavy trailer behind a motorhome's relatively short wheelbase (158") create enormous stress on the hitch point — exactly where sway begins.

🛑 Brake Fade on Descents

Long descents cause brake overheating and fade. When the tow vehicle's brakes are compromised, the trailer can begin to push the motorhome — a condition known as "trailer push" that rapidly induces sway. A WDH system that keeps the trailer tracking straight and the combined rig balanced is the first line of defense against catastrophic loss of control.

Common User-Reported Considerations

Weight distribution strength: Some users note the Andersen transfers less weight to the front axle than heavy spring bar systems (1,400+ lb bars). However, for the Solera 22N4X4's heavy E450 chassis, this is actually a benefit — the motorhome doesn't need aggressive weight transfer.

Initial bracket setup: Several users report that positioning the frame brackets correctly takes patience. Once dialed in, daily hookup is fast and repeatable. Drilling set-screw holes or welding brackets eliminates any future bracket movement.

Chain tension on uneven ground: When hitching at a campsite where the motorhome and trailer aren't aligned, chains may be harder to tension. The simple fix: pull straight first, then tension.

Not for ultra-light tongue weights: Andersen recommends at least 300 lbs of tongue weight for the friction cone to engage. A 5,000–6,000 lb enclosed trailer (500–900 lb tongue weight) is well within the optimal range.
